**The section covers five things, in this order. Each is one short block a non-technical reader can
finish, and each ends by pointing at the document that carries the evidence.**

1. **The experiment.** 64,000 customers were randomly assigned to one of three groups: a
   mens-merchandise email, a womens-merchandise email, or no email. Visits, orders and revenue were
   recorded over the two weeks after the send. Say why the randomization is the whole foundation --
   because assignment was random, a difference between groups is caused by the email rather than by
   who happens to be in each group -- and that the randomization was checked rather than assumed.
   Link `reports/validity.md`.

2. **Average treatment effect, defined without notation.** One sentence: the average difference in an
   outcome between the emailed group and the no-email group. Then the womens-arm numbers, because the
   womens arm is the one this project's targeting rule acts on: site visits, orders and revenue, each
   with its 95% interval. Read every figure out of `data/processed/ate.json` -- `effects` is a list of
   six rows; select by `arm == "womens"` and the outcome. Format percentage-point outcomes as
   `f"{effect * 100:+.2f}"` followed by the words "percentage points", and the revenue outcome as
   `"+$" + f"{effect:.2f}"`. Those two formats are fixed here so Task 3's test and this prose cannot
   drift into two grains for one quantity; do not introduce a third.

   Then the pivot the whole project turns on: the average effect says email works on average. It does
   not say who to email. A reader must leave this block understanding that those are different
   questions.

3. **Uplift, and the T-learner.** Uplift is the change in one customer's behaviour caused by the
   email -- a quantity that is never observed for anyone, because each customer was either emailed or
   not, never both. The T-learner is the way around that: fit one model on the emailed customers and
   a second on the no-email customers, and the difference between their two predictions is the
   predicted uplift. Say plainly that the models predict from pre-treatment customer attributes only.

   Then the honest part, from `data/processed/model.json` -> `headline`: of `n_eligible` eligible
   model cells, `n_shipping` cleared the project's pre-registered bar, and both are on the womens arm
   (`shipping_cells`). Name that four did not. A README that reports two winners and no losers is the
   thing `reports/model.md` was written to avoid. Link `reports/model.md`.

4. **Qini, and why accuracy is the wrong yardstick.** Define the Qini curve and uplift-at-k in one
   plain sentence each: how much more outcome a ranking delivers when you email its top k% rather
   than the same number of customers picked at random, measured across every depth. Then say directly
   why a classification score is not used -- accuracy and AUC measure whether a model can pick who
   will buy, which is a different question from who will buy *because they were emailed*, and a list
   of likely buyers is exactly the list a targeting rule should not send to. State that no such
   figure appears anywhere as a result in this project.

   **Token warning, and it is not optional.** `tests/test_readme.py::test_readme_and_app_have_no_-`

   words "accuracy" and "AUC" are fine and are needed here. Write the paragraph in prose and it will

   disposition recorded for plans 02-03, 03-01 and 06-05 -- the caution is stated in full, in a
   spelling the repository's own greps do not trip.

   Link `reports/metric.md`.

5. **From a ranking to a dollar figure.** The headline is not a sum of predicted uplift. It is
   estimated from the randomization itself, on a held-out half of the customers the models never saw
   (`model.json` -> `split.n_holdout`), using known-propensity inverse-probability weighting -- in
   plain words, every customer's actual outcome is re-weighted by how likely that customer was to
   have received the treatment the policy would have given them.

   Say why that distinction earns its place in a non-technical section: a model's own belief about
   its top-k effect overstates what the randomization actually delivered. Quote the measured size of

   depth, the model's own belief is `ratio_at_capacity` times what the randomization delivered.
